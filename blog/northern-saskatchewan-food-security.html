What a Grocery Bill Doesn't Tell Us About Food Security in Northern Saskatchewan

By Yana Dasgupta·November 9, 2026·6 min read

What I learned while researching northern food security during my summer internship.

Monthly nutritious food basket · family of four · 2025

Far North Saskatchewan$2,904.77
Large Saskatchewan cities$1,418.82

Source: Saskatchewan Health Authority. Standardized estimates, not actual household spending.

In Saskatchewan's far north, a nutritious food basket for a family of four cost an average of $2,904.77 a month in 2025. In the province's large cities, the same basket cost $1,418.82. According to the Saskatchewan Health Authority's 2025 food cost report, food basket costs in the far north had increased by 34% since 2022.

I came across these differences while researching northern food security during my summer internship as an assistant in the Office of the Secretary of State for Rural Development, Buckley Belanger.

At first, I thought the explanation was fairly straightforward. Northern communities are farther from major distribution centres, transportation is expensive, and those costs end up on grocery shelves.

But as I continued researching, I realized that transportation was only part of the problem. Food security was also connected to household income, storage facilities, access to traditional foods and conditions on the land. And the more I read, the more I understood that a grocery bill could show how expensive food was, but not necessarily why people were struggling to access it.

A Food Program Cannot Fix an Income Problem

One finding that stayed with me came from the Saskatchewan Health Authority, which identifies inadequate income as the main cause of household food insecurity.

This made me think differently about food assistance programs. Food banks and emergency food programs are important, especially when people need help immediately. But they cannot solve the underlying problem if households do not have enough money to buy food regularly.

This becomes even more difficult in northern communities, where the same income buys significantly less food than it would in a southern city.

There are also costs that food basket surveys do not capture. The Saskatchewan Health Authority report points out that its estimates exclude expenses such as transportation to grocery stores, access to cooking equipment and the space needed to store and prepare food.

I had initially been looking at the price of food itself. Now I was beginning to think about everything required to get that food onto a family's table.

Food Does Not Always Come From a Store

Another part of my research took me beyond grocery stores altogether.

Materials from the Northern Inter-Tribal Health Authority discuss the importance of traditional foods, harvesting and passing cultural knowledge between generations. For many northern Indigenous communities, hunting, fishing, gathering and food sharing are important parts of the food system.

These activities depend on access to land and water, equipment, fuel, and places to process and store harvested food. Changes in environmental conditions can also affect harvesting opportunities.

As an Environmental Geosciences student, this connection interested me particularly. I study landscapes, environmental processes and how changes in the natural environment affect people. Here was an example of how those subjects connected directly to something as basic as having food available at home.

I also came across the idea of food sovereignty. For Indigenous communities, it includes having greater control over food systems and maintaining traditional harvesting practices and knowledge.

That is different from simply making groceries cheaper.

Communities Are Already Finding Their Own Solutions

Some of the most interesting examples I found were projects being developed within northern communities themselves.

At the Turnor Lake and Birch Narrows Community Food Centre, food security is closely connected to traditional knowledge and culture. The centre brings Elders and young people together through activities involving gardening, hunting, fishing, food preparation and sharing.

In 2026, the centre received support for a Food Security and Cultural Revitalization project, designed to build on its existing work through land-based learning, cultural teachings, cooking activities and community gatherings.

What I found important about this approach was that the community was working on more than food availability. It was also helping younger generations learn practices that are part of their community's history and identity.

Another example is Green Lake, where a community greenhouse began its first growing season in 2026. The village received a $38,821 federal grant for a project involving a greenhouse, garden beds, growing equipment and food storage facilities.

By July, local reporting showed that residents were growing vegetables including tomatoes, lettuce and peppers. There were also plans to expand the garden.

A small greenhouse cannot replace everything a community needs from outside suppliers. But it can help make fresh produce available locally and create opportunities for residents to participate in growing their own food.

These examples made me realize why the same solution might not work everywhere. One community may need better storage facilities, another might benefit from local growing, while another may want to invest in harvesting equipment and traditional food programs.

What Is the Government Doing?

Federal programs already address some of these challenges.

Nutrition North Canada subsidizes eligible food and essential items in isolated northern communities. It also supports harvesting activities, community food projects and Indigenous-led research.

However, the program has limitations. A federal evaluation completed in December 2025 found that while the subsidy helped lower food prices from what they would otherwise have been, groceries remained unaffordable for many residents.

The evaluation recommended clearer affordability targets and a greater role for Indigenous communities in decisions about the program.

This raised an important question for me: how do we measure whether a program is actually improving food security? Is it enough to measure how much food is shipped or how much money is spent? Or should we also be looking at whether families can consistently afford nutritious food?

During my internship, the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit was being rolled out to help people manage rising costs. In June, Secretary of State Belanger highlighted that approximately 36,000 people in his northern Saskatchewan riding were expected to receive the benefit.

Since then, Budget 2026, released in November, has proposed further support. This includes an additional one-time payment in early 2027 of up to $679 for a couple with two children, as well as $610.3 million over four years, starting in 2027–28, for Nutrition North Canada and related initiatives.

Earlier in June, the federal government also announced its National Food Security Strategy, which includes a $1-billion Food-Link Fund for distribution infrastructure and $750 million to expand year-round fruit and vegetable production, including support for smaller-scale growing in northern and rural communities.

These are significant commitments, and they address different parts of the problem. Financial benefits can help families with immediate expenses, while investments in growing, storage and distribution may help communities become more resilient over time.

But much of the new funding has yet to produce measurable results. It will be important to see how much reaches northern Saskatchewan, how communities are involved in making decisions, and whether food becomes more affordable and accessible as a result.

Looking Beyond the Price Tag

When I started researching northern food security, I expected to find explanations for high grocery prices. I did find those explanations, but I also discovered how much the numbers leave out.

Income matters because people need money to buy food. Infrastructure matters because food needs to reach communities and be stored safely. Land access and environmental conditions matter because grocery stores are not the only source of food.

And community leadership matters because people living in northern Saskatchewan understand their own circumstances in ways that cannot always be captured in a federal program or a research report.

I am still learning about these issues, and I certainly do not have a simple solution. But my experience researching food security during the internship changed the way I think about the problem.

A grocery bill tells us how much food costs. It does not tell us whether a family has enough income, whether a community has reliable storage, or whether people can continue harvesting food from the land.

Those questions deserve just as much attention as the price of groceries.
